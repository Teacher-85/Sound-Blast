⚡ Level 1
True or False?
8 pts
1.Sound waves require a medium (matter) to travel through.
Sound is a mechanical wave — it transfers energy by making particles vibrate. Particles only exist in matter (solids, liquids, gases) — not in a vacuum. ✅ TRUE — No medium = no particles = no sound. This is why space is silent.
2.In a longitudinal wave, particles move at right angles to the direction of energy transfer.
In a longitudinal wave, particles vibrate parallel to (in the same direction as) energy transfer. Moving at right angles describes a transverse wave (like a wave on a rope). ✅ FALSE — Sound is longitudinal: particles vibrate back and forth, the same direction the wave travels.
3.A sound with a higher frequency will be perceived as louder.
Frequency → determines pitch (how high or low a note sounds). Amplitude → determines loudness (how much energy the wave carries). ✅ FALSE — Higher frequency = higher pitch, NOT louder. Don't mix these two up!
4.Sound travels faster in steel than in water or air.
Speed of sound: Air ≈ 340 m/s | Water ≈ 1500 m/s | Steel ≈ 5960 m/s In solids, particles are tightly packed — vibrations pass between them much faster. ✅ TRUE — Sound travels fastest in solids because the particles are closest together.
5.An echo occurs when a sound wave is reflected off a hard surface.
When a sound wave hits a hard, flat surface, it bounces back toward the source. If enough time passes before the reflected wave arrives, we perceive it as a separate sound — an echo. ✅ TRUE — Echoes are simply reflected sound waves.
6.Ultrasound has a frequency above 20 000 Hz — beyond the upper limit of human hearing.
Human hearing range: approximately 20 Hz – 20 000 Hz. "Ultra" literally means beyond. Ultrasound sits above this range — dogs, bats and dolphins can hear it; we can't. ✅ TRUE — Ultrasound is inaudible to humans because its frequency is too high.
7.Sound travels faster in seawater than in fresh water, because seawater is denser.
Seawater contains dissolved salt, making it denser than fresh water. Denser liquid → particles closer together → vibrations transferred between particles more efficiently. Speed of sound: Fresh water ≈ 1480 m/s | Seawater ≈ 1520 m/s ✅ TRUE — The higher density of seawater allows sound to travel slightly faster.
8.Increasing the amplitude of a sound wave will increase its frequency.
Amplitude and frequency are completely independent properties of a wave. Amplitude = height of the wave = loudness. Frequency = number of waves per second = pitch. You can have a loud, low-pitched sound (high amplitude, low frequency) — like a bass drum. ✅ FALSE — Amplitude and frequency are independent. Changing one does NOT change the other.

9.A guitar string is plucked harder. What change does this produce in the sound?
Plucking harder puts more energy into the string → bigger vibration → larger amplitude. Amplitude controls loudness — more amplitude = louder sound. ❌ A: Frequency depends on the string's length/tension, not how hard you pluck. ❌ D: The speed of sound in air stays the same (~340 m/s) regardless of amplitude. ✅ C — More force → bigger amplitude → louder sound. Frequency and speed are unchanged.
10.Which of the following correctly describes a region of rarefaction in a sound wave?
Compression = particles bunched up, higher pressure. Think of squeezing a spring together. Rarefaction = particles spread out, lower pressure. Think of stretching a spring apart. ❌ A describes a compression, not rarefaction. ❌ C is wrong — particles in a sound wave vibrate back and forth but don't travel with the wave permanently. ✅ D — Rarefaction = particles spread apart, lower than normal pressure.
11.Why does sound travel faster in seawater than in fresh water?
Dissolved salt increases seawater's density — particles are slightly closer together than in fresh water. Closer particles = faster transfer of vibrations = faster wave speed. ❌ B: Temperature does affect speed, but this isn't always true — seawater isn't always warmer. ❌ D: Electrical conductivity is completely unrelated to sound transmission. ✅ A — Greater density → particles closer → vibrations pass between them more quickly.
12.A student claps once while standing 170 m from a cliff face. How long after the clap will they hear the echo? (Speed of sound in air = 340 m/s)
The sound must travel TO the cliff AND back — total distance = 170 × 2 = 340 m Using: time = distance ÷ speed time = 340 ÷ 340 = 1.0 second ❌ A (0.5 s): Common mistake — only counting one-way distance (170 m). The sound travels there AND back! ✅ B — Total distance is 340 m (there and back). 340 ÷ 340 = 1.0 second.
13.In an ultrasound scan, what happens when the sound pulse reaches a boundary between two different types of tissue?
At a boundary between materials of different density (e.g. muscle and kidney), a sound wave partially reflects and partially transmits. The reflected pulse returns to the probe. The time delay tells the machine how deep the boundary is. The transmitted portion continues and reflects off the next boundary — building up a full image layer by layer. ✅ C — Partial reflection at each boundary is exactly what allows ultrasound to map internal structures.
14.Which wave property directly determines the pitch of a sound?
Frequency = number of complete waves per second (Hz). High frequency → many waves per second → high pitched sound (e.g. a whistle ~2000 Hz). Low frequency → fewer waves per second → low pitched sound (e.g. a bass note ~80 Hz). ❌ A: Amplitude → loudness. ❌ B: Wave speed doesn't directly determine perceived pitch. ✅ D — Frequency is the direct cause of pitch. Higher frequency = higher pitch.

24.A sound wave has a frequency of 256 Hz and a wavelength of 1.33 m. Calculate its wave speed.
Given: f = 256 Hz  |  λ = 1.33 m  |  Find: v = ?
v = f × λ
v = m/s
Formula: v = f × λ

Substitute: v = 256 × 1.33

Calculate: v = 340.48 m/s

📌 This is very close to the speed of sound in air at room temperature (~340 m/s) — a good reality check!
25.A sound wave travels at 1500 m/s in seawater and has a wavelength of 0.75 m. Calculate its frequency.
Given: v = 1500 m/s  |  λ = 0.75 m  |  Find: f = ?
v = f × λ  →  f = v ÷ λ
f = Hz
Formula: v = f × λ   →   rearrange to find f:   f = v ÷ λ

Substitute: f = 1500 ÷ 0.75

Calculate: f = 2000 Hz

📌 Rearranging tip: if v = f × λ, cover up what you want to find. f = v ÷ λ, λ = v ÷ f.
26.A SONAR pulse is sent from a ship and the echo returns 2.4 seconds later. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in seawater. Calculate the depth of the ocean below the ship.
💡 Hint: The sound travels DOWN to the seabed AND back UP. The depth is only half the total distance.
Given: time = 2.4 s  |  speed = 1500 m/s  |  Find: depth = ?
distance = speed × time  →  depth = distance ÷ 2
depth = m
Step 1 — Total distance travelled by sound:
distance = speed × time = 1500 × 2.4 = 3600 m

Step 2 — Depth (sound only travels one way to reach the seabed):
depth = 3600 ÷ 2 = 1800 m

📌 The most common mistake here is forgetting to halve the total distance. The echo path is there AND back — depth is only one way!
27.A bat produces ultrasound at a frequency of 50 000 Hz. Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. Calculate the wavelength of this sound.
Given: f = 50 000 Hz  |  v = 340 m/s  |  Find: λ = ?
v = f × λ  →  λ = v ÷ f
λ = m
Formula: v = f × λ   →   rearrange: λ = v ÷ f

Substitute: λ = 340 ÷ 50 000

Calculate: λ = 0.0068 m (or 6.8 mm)

📌 Notice how tiny this wavelength is! Short wavelengths are better at detecting small objects — this is why ultrasound at high frequency gives detailed images.

28.Explain, using particle theory, why sound cannot travel through a vacuum but CAN travel through a solid, liquid, or gas. [3 marks]
Mark 1 Sound is a mechanical wave — it transfers energy by causing particles to vibrate.
Mark 2 In a solid, liquid, or gas, there are particles present that can be made to vibrate and pass the energy along from one particle to the next.
Mark 3 In a vacuum there are no particles at all — with nothing to vibrate, the wave has no way to transfer energy, so sound simply cannot travel through it.

💡 Common mistake: saying "sound needs air." It actually needs any medium — it travels through water and steel too!
29.Describe how a bat uses echolocation to locate a moth flying in complete darkness. [3 marks]
Mark 1 The bat emits short pulses of ultrasound (high-frequency sound above 20 000 Hz) from its mouth or nose.
Mark 2 The pulse travels through the air and reflects off the moth, returning as an echo to the bat's large ears.
Mark 3 The bat detects the time delay between emitting the pulse and receiving the echo — the shorter the delay, the closer the moth. The brain processes this to pinpoint the exact location and distance of the prey.

💡 Bats can do this so fast and accurately they can even catch insects mid-flight in total darkness.
30.A doctor uses ultrasound to image a patient's kidney. Explain how the machine produces a detailed image. Refer to what is emitted, what happens at tissue boundaries, and how the image is formed. [4 marks]
Mark 1 The probe emits short pulses of ultrasound (high-frequency sound waves above 20 000 Hz) into the body.
Mark 2 When pulses reach a boundary between tissues of different densities (e.g. muscle and kidney wall), some of the sound is reflected back as an echo; the rest continues deeper.
Mark 3 The probe detects the returning echoes and a computer records the time delay for each echo — a longer delay means the boundary is deeper in the body.
Mark 4 By processing the timing and strength of thousands of echoes, the computer builds up a detailed image of the internal structures on screen in real time.

💡 This is identical in principle to SONAR — both use reflected sound pulses and measure time delays to map what they can't see directly.
31.Compare a transverse wave and a longitudinal wave. In your answer, describe how the particles move in each, give a real-world example of each, and state which type sound is. [4 marks]
Mark 1 In a transverse wave, particles vibrate perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer. Example: a wave on a rope, or a water wave.
Mark 2 In a longitudinal wave, particles vibrate parallel (in the same direction) as energy transfer, creating alternating compressions and rarefactions.
Mark 3 Example of a longitudinal wave: sound travelling through air, water, or a solid.
Mark 4 Sound is a longitudinal wave — the air particles are pushed back and forth in the same direction as the wave travels, forming compressions and rarefactions.

💡 Memory tip: Longitudinal = "long" — particles move in the same direction as the long axis of travel.
